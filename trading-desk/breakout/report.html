Breakout Backtest

The book's flat-base breakout, as fixed rules on daily bars, tested on today's S&P 500 stocks from 2014 (after the book came out) to October 2026. About 22,000 trades, every one re-checked by an independent audit.

VERDICT · NO EDGE OVER RANDOM

It makes a small profit after costs, but buying the same stocks on random days with the same stop and exits did just as well. The pattern lost to random entries in 2014–2020 and beat them from 2021, so there's no steady advantage. The book's market and Bollinger filters made it worse.

The setup

  1. Scan: price $2–2,000, average volume over 400k shares, up over the last month and above its 50-day average.
  2. Base: a 15-day range no deeper than 12%, with the top tested at least twice and the close within 3% under it.
  3. Entry: buy stop just above the top, good for one day and renewed while the base holds.
  4. Stop: just under the 10-day low.
  5. Exits: half the position targets 1R and is sold if it hasn't got there in 3 days ("up or out"). The other half targets 2R and trails under the 5-day low once 1R is hit. Anything left after 20 days is sold.

Net result per trade, by period

After 5 bps slippage each side. One R is the planned risk, entry to stop.

The random control buys the next open on about 1 in 20 days that pass the same scan. It shows what today's S&P 500 gave anyone holding it for a few weeks. The pattern has to beat that to show skill.

What happens to the trades

  • Breakouts rarely run. Only 7% of first halves reach 1R. 87% are sold after 3 days for not moving ("up or out").
  • Half the trades are winners. 51% of trades end positive, and the median trade is held 20 days.
  • Stops are wide. The median stop is 5.4% below entry, so costs are small, about 0.02R per trade.
  • The book's filters cut trades in half and the edge with them: +0.014R per trade, not significant in either period.

All runs

Worst and best only differ on days where a candle touches both the stop and a target, so you can't tell which came first. On daily bars with wide stops this barely matters.

What this test does and doesn't cover
  • Survivorship: only today's S&P 500 members, each traded only after it joined the index. Stocks that dropped out are missing, which flatters every arm. The random control carries the same bias, which is why it's the yardstick.
  • The author draws triangles, flags and trend lines by eye, and fine-tunes entries on 5–60 minute charts. These fixed rules capture the flat-base core, not his judgement.
  • Targets are 1R and 2R instead of his Fibonacci levels; his two worked trades had targets at 0.5–0.9R and 1.4–2.5R.
  • Not modelled: skipping stocks with earnings or FDA news coming up. No free earnings-date history was available.
  • Trades are measured one at a time, with no limit on how many positions are open together.
